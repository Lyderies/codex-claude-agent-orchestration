# Spend Opus on ambiguity, not effort

As of 31 October 2026, a deterministic orchestrator that applies "quality first, then subscription usage, then quota headroom" should keep **Claude Opus 5.5** as its judgment model but run it at **medium effort by default**. Single scoped coding tasks go to **Sonnet 5.5 at medium**, and bounded mechanical edits and codebase exploration go to **Haiku 5.5**. **GPT-6.1 Sol** is the cross-vendor reviewer and the alternative in almost every row. **GPT-6 Astra** is first only for security review, and **Fable 5.1** stays a budgeted last resort. Opus at high effort is not too expensive for planning in absolute terms. On a provisional calibration, a plan costs about **0.6–2.7% of a Max 5x session at high against 0.4–1.5% at medium**. But no measurement shows that high-effort plans are better, and higher effort amplifies the overruns and overbuilding that practitioners report most often. So medium is the planning default, and high is kept for ambiguous, architectural or irreversible plans. For scoped coding, Opus at high is plainly too costly. In two counted studies it spends **roughly three to four times Sonnet medium's usage per small task for identical pass rates**, and high is the first Opus setting that broke passing tests in a 300-run study of real merged changes. Usage estimates now rest on explicit formulas. A ChatGPT Plus five-hour window holds roughly **100 Codex credits**, and a small GPT-6.1 Sol task measured 1–2% of it. Anthropic publishes no Claude session size; one Pro-plan measurement implies about **$36 of API-equivalent usage per Pro session, or about $180 per Max 5x session**, and every installation must recalibrate that locally. Community preference, tallied from about 30 voices, enters only as small pseudo-counts worth 0.6–6 paired runs per row. It can order the first experiments and settle lopsided rows, but it cannot settle any contested one. Confidence is medium for the coding rows and low for planning, research, documentation, security and review.

## Opus at high effort buys nothing measurable on scoped work

Three independent counted studies agree that small, well-specified, test-backed tasks saturate. The first was a 48-run comparison on an 11-file repository, with four tasks: a rounding bug, a five-file rename, a pricing script and a duplicated-validation extraction. **Sonnet 5.5 at medium and Opus 5.5 at medium both passed 12 of 12 runs.** Sonnet's median cost was **$0.074 per run against $0.162**, and it was 39% faster; moving Sonnet to high "gave no measurable gain" ([wmedia](https://wmedia.es/en/tips/claude-code-sonnet-5-5-vs-opus-5-5-benchmark)). The second was a 182-run study of seven scoped tasks on a 226-test repository. **Haiku 5.5 passed 70 of 70 runs at both low and medium effort**, Sonnet and Opus each passed 21 of 21, and exploration cost about **40 times more on Opus** at list price (DEV Community study; see the method note). The third and most realistic was a 300-run study of six changes that had actually been merged into a large TypeScript monorepo. Each change touched up to 26 files and was graded by the tests the original fix shipped with. Every model fixed 83–94% of the new tests at every effort level, so the differences lay in what else broke. **Sonnet 5.5 broke a passing test in 0 of 100 runs. Opus 5.5 broke none at low or medium, one each at high and xhigh, and two at max, and it dropped instructions on the same schedule.** GPT-6 Sol broke passing tests in 27 of 100 runs (the "The Default Was Right" effort study; see the method note).

The cost side is just as consistent. Opus at medium cost **2.2 times** Sonnet medium per run on the small tasks, and **3.7 times** on the merged changes ($2.21 against $0.59 per run). Moving Opus from medium to high adds another **40–80%**: 36% on Cursor's live CursorBench board ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/en-US/evals)), 48% on the merged changes, and about 80% per step when Artificial Analysis's tenfold low-to-max spread for Opus 5.5 ($0.98 to $10.04 per task) is divided evenly across its four steps ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/artificial-analysis-intelligence-index-v5)). Opus at high therefore spends **about three to four times Sonnet medium's usage on small scoped tasks and five to eight times on multi-file ones**. It buys no extra passes, and it is the first Opus setting that regresses working code. Under a rule that settles quality ties on cost, that is too costly. The merged-change study's author reached the same verdict: Opus "peaks at medium", and effort is "a spend dial".

"Scoped" has to mean unambiguous and automatically checkable, not merely small. CursorBench 4.0 is built from "ambiguous, multi-file tasks from real Cursor sessions", and there **Opus medium scores 52.5% against 39.2% for Sonnet medium and 47.8% for Sonnet high** ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/en-US/evals)). A task that needs investigation or intent inference therefore belongs to Opus medium, even when it is a single ticket. The same board shows **Haiku 5.5 at high (42.3% for $0.32) beating Sonnet at medium (39.2% for $0.52)**. That makes Haiku high a credible cheap alternative for narrow scoped edits, provided the context stays under the 100K-token mark where Haiku's price rises fivefold ([Haiku 5.5 migration guide](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/models/haiku-5-5/migration-guide)).

GPT-6.1 Sol has no controlled head-to-head at medium or high on scoped tasks. The one structured single-task comparison scored Sol and Opus equal on spec adherence, at a cost of **$0.27 against $1.11** ([DataCamp](https://www.datacamp.com/blog/gpt-6-1-sol-vs-opus-5-5)). The regression rate above belongs to GPT-6 Sol, not 6.1. Sol is a sound scoped worker when Codex has more headroom, but only behind a full-test-suite gate.

Planning has **no plan-quality benchmark for any of these models**, so the decision rests on proxies. Anthropic says Opus 5.5 "remains clearly stronger at complex, open-ended work requiring sustained judgment" ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-sonnet-5-5)), and Claude Code's `opusplan` alias plans with Opus and executes with Sonnet ([Claude Code model config](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/model-config)). Neither says anything about effort, and the proxies that do are mixed. CursorBench's intent-understanding mix is the closest proxy for planning plus doing, and there Opus gains **3.5 points from medium to high for $1.06 more per task, and nothing from high to xhigh** for another $3.01 ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/en-US/evals)); Cursor itself warns that small gaps may not be meaningful. On the merged changes Opus peaked at medium, and on SlopCodeBench Opus at xhigh produced fewer strict passes than at medium (25 against 28) at up to 4.22 times the cost ([daily.dev summary of SlopCodeBench](https://daily.dev/posts/slopcodebench-10-2026-opus-5-5-astra-wins-overall-xhigh-considered-harmful-mjhhblw2w)). Practitioners describe the planning risk as overrun, not weak reasoning. Every's reviewers warn that "Opus will take the afternoon if you let it" ([Every](https://every.to/vibe-check/vibe-check-opus-5-5-is-pulling-our-codex-converts-back-to-claude)). A widely read Hacker News thread complains that generated plans are "way too much prose" and that planning "wastes a ton of tokens" ([Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49840054)). Published orchestration configurations that state an effort run their Opus orchestrator at medium.

In absolute terms the premium is small, because a plan runs once per feature. On the provisional calibration developed below, an Opus plan costs **$0.7–2.7 of API-equivalent usage at medium (0.4–1.5% of a Max 5x session) and $1.0–4.8 at high (0.6–2.7%)**. Opus at high is therefore affordable for planning but not justified as the default. It buys a borderline proxy gain for 40–80% more usage, along with more of the failure mode that actually hurts. The table uses **medium for routine plans and high when requirements are ambiguous, architectural or irreversible**, which is where the intent-understanding proxy shows its gain. It always sends a time budget, because Opus 5.5 "paces its work to finish inside the budget" when the harness reports elapsed time ([Prompting Opus 5.5](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-opus-5-5)).

Sonnet does not take over planning. It trails Opus on every knowledge- and judgment-sensitive evaluation available, including AA-Omniscience (30 against 43–48) and HLE-Diamond (53% against 64–68%) ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/comparisons/claude-opus-5-5-xhigh-vs-claude-opus-5-5)). On Anthropic's FrontierCode chart, **Opus at medium (54.6% for $0.80) beats Sonnet's best result (52.1% at xhigh for $1.59)** ([Tess](https://tess.im/blog/claude-sonnet-5-5-cheap-below-max)). Sonnet's cost also climbs steeply above high: xhigh costs five to eight times Sonnet medium, both on CursorBench and on the merged changes.

After Cursor repriced Sonnet on 7 October, Sonnet xhigh and Opus medium sit level on CursorBench ($2.81 against $2.91, at 53.1% against 52.5%). That makes the widely quoted $3.88 launch-chart figure for Sonnet xhigh stale ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/en-US/evals); [Digital Applied](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/sonnet-5-5-or-opus-5-5-effort-level-cost-per-task)). On real merged changes, however, Sonnet xhigh cost $4.80 per run for 10 clean runs in 20, against $2.21 and 13 clean runs for Opus medium. On hard work, Opus medium is the cheaper way to buy quality. Sonnet at max is dominated everywhere it was measured, and on the merged changes it cost $20.76 per run.

Each cheaper model therefore takes a specific seat. **Sonnet 5.5 at medium** takes scoped, test-backed implementation and fully specified migrations, where it leads Vals Code Migration 87.4% to 80.5% ([Vals Index](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/vals_index)), and Sonnet at high takes authored documentation. **Haiku 5.5** takes mechanical edits, read-only exploration and narrow scoped tasks under 100K tokens of context. **GPT-6.1 Sol** takes any row where the quality evidence is tied and the Codex pool has more headroom; it is also the default reviewer of Claude-written work and the cross-vendor planning alternative at high, although current threads use GPT models more often to review Opus plans than to write them ([Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49840054)). Anthropic's advisor pattern offers a middle path worth a paired run. Anthropic reports that Sonnet consulting an Opus advisor scored higher on SWE-bench Multilingual than Sonnet alone and cost less per task ([Anthropic](https://claude.com/blog/the-advisor-strategy)).

## The revised table runs Opus at medium and adds two rows

The table applies the routing rule literally. A candidate wins on quality only when independent evidence shows a gap larger than benchmark noise (about 3 Terminal-Bench points) or several sources agree in direction; otherwise the candidates are tied, and usage decides, then headroom. Usage means the **expected change in the binding window's used percentage per accepted task**, so a cheap model that needs two attempts can lose to a dearer one that needs one ([hslab](https://hslab.space/en/blog/posts/gpt-6-1-sol-vs-opus-sonnet-5-5/)). Before the ordering applies, an availability gate blocks any dispatch into an exhausted window, any dispatch that would push a pool past the 85% point at which Claude Code starts warning ([Claude Code errors](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/errors)), and any dispatch that would dip into the reserved escalation budget. Effort is set per provider and always passed explicitly, because Claude Code runs Sonnet 5.5 one level lower than the raw API by default ([The Batch](https://www.deeplearning.ai/the-batch/claude-fills-out-its-model-family/)).

### Why each row lands where it does

The quality ordering comes from harness-inclusive agentic benchmarks. On Terminal-Bench 4.0, Claude leads GPT-6.1 Sol by 6.6 points on the public board and by 10 points on Vals. Both gaps are larger than the roughly ±3-point margins on the top entries ([CodingFleet](https://codingfleet.com/blog/terminal-bench-4-leaderboard-2026/); [BenchLM](https://benchlm.ai/benchmarks/terminal-bench-4)). The Vals run also supplies the per-task cost and time figures this report uses as multipliers.

**Planning, scoped coding and substantial coding.** These rows follow from the previous section. One point applies to substantial work in particular. The merged-change study, with changes of up to 26 files, favours Opus medium (13 of 20 clean runs, no regressions) over high (12 of 20, one regression). CursorBench favours high by 3.5 points, and Claude Code's effort guide reserves high for "bug fixes where verification matters" ([Claude Code model config](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/model-config)). Medium is therefore the default, and high is both the first escalation and the setting for unknown-cause debugging. Escalating effort inside a running child is cheap, because Opus 5.5 can change effort mid-session without resetting the prompt cache ([Claude blog](https://claude.com/blog/claude-opus-5-5-built-for-coding-sessions-that-use-more-context)). Side-by-side practitioner reports still favour Opus on bug severity, with "fewer high severity bugs and … fewer rounds of fixes" than Sol ([Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49897054)). Sol stays the cross-vendor alternative for when the Claude pool is gated.

**Mechanical edits and exploration.** Here the measured Haiku parity carries the decision. Haiku stays at medium by default because Anthropic found that moving it from low to medium "roughly halved early stopping" under long agent prompts ([Prompting Haiku 5.5](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-haiku-5-5)). Low is acceptable only when a deterministic verifier catches false completions; in the Haiku study, low reached the same accepted result with 140K tokens against 233K. Configurations are nearly unanimous on a cheap tier for search and exploration, under the rationale that "search needs speed, not intelligence" ([oh-my-openagent docs](https://ohmyopenagent.com/docs)).

Luna stays out of agentic edits. It scores 13.6% on Vals Terminal-Bench, and practitioners report that it "leaks tool calls and markers in the output" ([Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49897054)). Terra runs in shadow. It is five days old, and on Terminal-Bench it cost $2.04 per task against Sol's $1.72, so its half-price tokens do not guarantee lower usage ([Vals AI](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/terminal-bench-4)).

**Research and documentation.** Deep research stays with Opus, because every knowledge-sensitive independent measure favours it. That includes Vals Legal Research, at 50.5% against 39.4% for Astra and 38.5% for Sol ([Vals Index](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/vals_index)). Anthropic also says Opus 5.5 is "much less likely to state an incorrect figure or cite the wrong source" ([Prompting Opus 5.5](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-opus-5-5)). Every's 80/20 Codex share for knowledge work keeps Sol a close alternative ([Every](https://every.to/vibe-check/vibe-check-opus-5-5-is-pulling-our-codex-converts-back-to-claude)).

Documentation shows no material quality gap among the strong models, so usage decides. On AA-Briefcase, Sonnet sits level with Opus (1814 against 1801) ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/comparisons/claude-opus-5-5-xhigh-vs-claude-opus-5-5)). Every prefers Astra for technical writing and found that Opus "buries the point", while another commenter calls Astra "simply bad at writing" ([Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50248124)), so Astra is an editor for public-facing documents rather than the default writer. Terra has the lowest hallucination rate in the GPT-6 family, 34.5% against 51.9% for Sol ([BenchLM](https://benchlm.ai/blog/posts/gpt-6-models-compared)), which makes it the most promising documentation challenger. Sonnet needs its scope instruction because it "tends to add tests, documentation, and small supporting files" unasked ([Prompting Sonnet 5.5](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-sonnet-5-5)).

**Security and review.** Security review keeps Astra first. On AA's DeepsecBench, GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6 Sol found bugs that require sequential reasoning in about 30% of runs, roughly twice the next-best models ([AA Cyber Index](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/artificial-analysis-cyber-index)). GPT-6.1 Sol, by contrast, ranks 45th of 47 on Vals CyberBench ([Vals GPT-6.1 Sol](https://www.vals.ai/models/openai_gpt-6.1-sol)), and both Sol and Opus have refused security work in practitioner reports ([Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50248124)). Anthropic reroutes most cyber tasks to Opus 4.8 ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-opus-5-5)), so the row is policy-gated rather than capability-ranked. The new element is a plan gate: on Plus, a full-surface Astra review can exceed a whole five-hour window (Table 3), so Astra reviews diffs there.

Review keeps the rule that the reviewer never comes from the writer's model family. On MacroscopeBench, an earlier Sol generation (GPT-5.6 Sol at high) matched Astra at max on F-score (77.8 against 78.0) at half the cost. In the same benchmark, Opus's recall rose with effort, from 57.4% at low to 76.6% at high, while precision stayed flat. That data comes from the previous-generation Opus 5 ([Macroscope](https://macroscope.com/content/ai-code-review-benchmark-best-models)), and it is why Opus reviews Codex-written work at high.

**Hard end-to-end tasks.** Opus stays first. Sonnet lands within a point of it on Vals Terminal-Bench, but at 1.25 times the cost and 1.28 times the time. Sonnet at medium also collapses on long-horizon work, scoring 28.8% against 57.6% for Opus medium on Anthropic's chart ([Tess](https://tess.im/blog/claude-sonnet-5-5-cheap-below-max)). That is why the in-pool alternative is Sonnet xhigh and never medium. Astra topped SlopCodeBench and was the only model whose strict passes rose at xhigh ([daily.dev summary of SlopCodeBench](https://daily.dev/posts/slopcodebench-10-2026-opus-5-5-astra-wins-overall-xhigh-considered-harmful-mjhhblw2w)). But one hard Astra task costs more than a whole Plus window, so it is the alternative only on Pro. Fable sits below both Opus and Astra on independent Terminal-Bench runs and costs about 1.3 times Opus per task. A Fable escalation is mostly a different-model retry, and it must be budgeted as one.

## Window shares, not API dollars, are the cost currency

Both vendors appear to meter subscriptions roughly in proportion to price-weighted tokens. OpenAI's Plus message estimates scale inversely with its credit rates: GPT-6.1 Terra gets about twice Sol's messages at half Sol's rate, and Luna about twenty times at a twentieth ([Codex pricing](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/pricing)). Astra is the exception, at about 3.3 times fewer messages for five times the rate, which is consistent with its leaner token use. On the Claude side, Anthropic cut Opus 5.5's list price by 20% and said it "goes 25% further within limits", which is exactly 1/0.8 ([Claude Devs on X](https://x.com/ClaudeDevs/status/2102438800836489554)). That is enough to turn a task's token profile into a share of a window, once each window's size is known in the same price-weighted units:

```text
Codex   credits = [P·(h·r_cached + (1−h)·r_in) + O·r_out] / 1e6
        share of a Plus 5-hour window = credits / B,   B ≈ 100 credits (60–200)
        GPT-6.1 Sol rates per 1M tokens: r_in 50, r_cached 2.5, r_out 250 credits (25 credits ≈ $1)

Claude  cost$ = [P·(f_read·0.20 + f_write1h·8 + f_uncached·4) + O·20] / 1e6 × m_model × m_effort
        share of a Max 5x session = cost$ / U5;   share of a Max 20x session = cost$ / (4·U5)
        U5 ≈ $180 provisional (at least $90–360); measure it locally

P = processed input tokens, h or f = cache shares, O = output tokens including reasoning

Per-task multipliers (relative to Opus 5.5 medium, or GPT-6.1 Sol medium on Codex)
  Sonnet 0.18–0.46 at medium on scoped and multi-file work, 1.25 on hard terminal work
  Haiku 0.025–0.25     Fable 1.2–1.5 (plus a cap of 50% of the weekly limit)
  Opus effort:   low 0.4–0.56, high 1.4–1.8, xhigh 2.4–3.2, max 4.6–7.0
  Sonnet effort: low 0.7, high 2.3, xhigh 5–8, max 14–35
  Astra 3.3–5.6 × Sol    Terra 0.5–1.2 × Sol    Luna 0.05–0.2 × Sol
  Sol effort on small tasks: low 0.9–1.0, high 1.0–1.3    Codex Fast 2.5, Ultrafast 8
```

**The Codex window.** The Codex window size B is anchored on a measured Plus test. A small order-aggregation task on GPT-6.1 Sol used **1–2% of the five-hour window per run, at both low and high effort**, and processed about 123K tokens with roughly 92% cache hits (note.com test; see the method note). At list credit rates, that is about 1.5–2 credits, which puts B between about 70 and 200, with 100 as the working value. The cross-check holds: OpenAI's estimate of 15–160 Sol messages per window implies 0.6–6.7 credits per message, which matches its "typical task" figure of 2–15 credits once that figure is halved for 6.1 Sol's rates ([Codex pricing](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/pricing)). B ≈ 100 credits equals about **$4 of API-equivalent usage per Plus window**. The same tester saw the weekly meter move only about 1% across all runs, so the weekly Plus budget is weakly bounded at 5–30 windows.

**The Claude session.** U5, the Max 5x session size, has no vendor figure and no direct 2026 measurement. One indirect anchor exists. A Claude Pro subscriber who instrumented their own pull requests reported about **10% of a five-hour window and $3.59 of API-equivalent usage per PR** when running Opus alone, and about **6% and $2.14 per PR** after moving implementation to Sonnet and Haiku subagents. Both ratios imply **about $36 of API-equivalent usage per Pro session** (personal blog post, 7 October; see the method note). Max 5x carries five times Pro's session allowance ([SessionWatcher](https://sessionwatcher.com/guides/is-claude-max-worth-it)), so U5 ≈ $180, and a Max 20x session ≈ $720. Anthropic's illustrative Opus 5.5 session (10M cache reads, 500K input, 200K output) costs $8.00 at list price ([BuildThisNow](https://www.buildthisnow.com/blog/guide/mechanics/claude-opus-5-5-in-claude-code)), or 4.4% of a Max 5x session on this calibration.

The anchor is weak for three reasons. It is one user on Pro. It predates complaints that Opus 5.5 limits felt stricter after about 13 October ([Claude Code issue mirror](https://claudeissues.com/issue/101937-bug-opus-5-5-usage-limits-exhausted-within-1-2-days)). And nobody knows whether the meter prices cache reads at the API ratio: "25% further" matched the input and output price cut, not the 60% cache-read cut. Hence the factor-of-two band, and the instruction to replace the figure locally.

**A cross-vendor implication.** Set beside the Codex figure, the calibration implies that a $20 Claude Pro session holds about nine times the API-equivalent usage of a $20 Plus window ($36 against $4). That gap is close to the per-task API-cost gap between the two families: Opus 5.5 costs 7.7 times GPT-6.1 Sol per Vals Terminal-Bench task ($13.20 against $1.72). If both figures hold, a hard task consumes a similar share of either $20 window, about 30–40%. A router that compared vendors in API dollars would then misroute systematically. This is a hypothesis built from two single-source measurements, and it is the first thing the calibration runs should test.

### Measuring the Claude session size locally

Measuring U5 takes a quiet window and two instruments. The first requirement is isolation: session and weekly pools are shared across claude.ai, Claude Code and IDE use, `/usage` counts only the local machine ([Claude Code costs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/costs)), and a set `ANTHROPIC_API_KEY` silently bills the API instead of the plan ([Claude Help](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/11145838-using-claude-code-with-your-pro-or-max-plan)). The meter instrument is the status-line JSON, read before and after each child run through the fields `rate_limits.five_hour.used_percentage` and `rate_limits.seven_day.used_percentage` ([Claude Code status line](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/statusline)). The token instrument records uncached input, 5-minute and 1-hour cache writes, cache reads and output for each run, from the session's usage records or from a ccusage-style `blocks` report, which groups usage into five-hour windows and prices it at API rates. Because the meter moves in whole percents, the calibration needs a fixed battery of 30–50 runs spread across Opus, Sonnet and Haiku at two effort levels, enough to move the session meter by at least 20 points. U5 is then the sum of API dollars divided by the sum of percentage changes, times 100, and a regression of each run's percentage change on its four token-type counts tests whether the meter weights cache reads at the API ratio, which is the main unknown. Running the same procedure against the seven-day field estimates the weekly budget, and reading Fable runs against the Fable weekly bar in Settings calibrates the Fable cap. The calibration should be repeated after any vendor limit announcement and at least monthly, since limits changed on 22 September and reportedly again in mid-October. The same recipe calibrates Codex. The app-server's `account/rateLimits/read` returns per-window `usedPercent`, `windowDurationMins` and `resetsAt`, and `account/usage/read` returns per-thread credit estimates by model, effort and speed ([openai/codex app-server](https://github.com/openai/codex/tree/main/codex-rs/app-server)). Identify Codex windows by duration, not by their primary or secondary label. Avoid polling `/status`, which one report says moves the five-hour meter on its own ([openai/codex#22040](https://github.com/openai/codex/issues/22040)).

### Overheads that move usage more than model choice

In several rows, caching, launch context and speed modes move per-task usage more than the choice of model does.

**Caching.** Measured agent sessions run at 90–97% cache hits. Falling from 97% to 80% roughly triples GPT-6.1 Sol's input cost. On Opus 5.5, moving from 92% to 80% reads, with more writes, roughly doubles it, because Claude's input cost is dominated by 1-hour cache writes at $8 per million tokens, not by reads at $0.20 ([Claude blog](https://claude.com/blog/claude-opus-5-5-built-for-coding-sessions-that-use-more-context)).

**Launch context.** A fresh child pays its startup context again. No source measured that context, but assuming 15–40K tokens of system prompt, tool schemas and instruction files, a fresh GPT-6.1 Sol context costs 0.75–2 credits, about **0.75–2% of a Plus window before any work starts**, so a five-way fan-out spends 4–10% of a Plus window on launch alone. A non-forked Opus subagent pays $0.12–0.32, while a forked one, which starts from the parent's cache, pays $0.003–0.008 ([Claude blog](https://claude.com/blog/claude-opus-5-5-built-for-coding-sessions-that-use-more-context)). The Haiku study found a median 47K-token launch prompt dominating each worker's cost. The Pro-plan study concluded that "the real saving is that the main session stays small".

**Model and effort switches.** Switching models in Claude Code re-reads the conversation with no cache hits ([Claude Code errors](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/errors)). The orchestrator should therefore escalate effort before switching model inside a running Claude child, and switch models only at task boundaries.

**Speed modes.** Codex Fast mode draws 2.5 times included usage, and Ultrafast 8 times ([Codex pricing](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/pricing)). Opus fast mode is billed at twice the API price, and it is unverified whether a Max plan draws it from plan allowance or from usage credits. Neither belongs in a default route.

**Plan facts that changed.** Two facts differ from early-October guidance. On Claude, a third-party check of a live Max 5x account on 19 September found exactly three meters (session, week and Fable week) and no separate Opus-only weekly cap ([tokn.watch](https://tokn.watch/blog/claude-weekly-limit/)), although Claude Code's error reference still documents "Opus limit" and "Sonnet limit" messages ([Claude Code errors](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/errors)). The orchestrator should therefore choose Sonnet for cost rather than as an escape from an Opus cap, while still parsing those messages; Max users may spend up to 50% of the weekly limit on Fable ([Claude on X](https://x.com/claudeai/status/2074548243971604641)). On Codex, Pro plans "currently have no five-hour limit" ([Codex pricing](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/pricing)), so their weekly window binds, and Pro allowances changed in contradictory ways around 29 October ([AIbase](https://news.aibase.com/news/31974); [Startup Fortune](https://startupfortune.com/openai-cuts-chatgpt-pro-200-usage-in-half-as-the-grace-period-ends-today/)). On Plus, one Astra automation session was reported to empty a five-hour window within minutes ([OpenAI community](https://community.openai.com/t/bug-codex-gpt-6-astra-desktop-automation-burning-100-of-5-hour-quota-in-5-minutes/1396450)).

## Community preference enters as a capped prior

The community tally counts stated preferences and published configurations. It does not measure quality. It covers 14 source documents and about 30 voices, mostly from Hacker News, GitHub configurations and practitioner blogs; no Reddit, Discord or YouTube content could be read. Each voice casts one vote per task type, split among the models it names as primary, with fallbacks not counted. Each vote is multiplied by a source-grade factor (3.0 for systematic measurements, 2.0 for hands-on comparisons, 1.5 for published configurations, 1.0 for anecdotes, 0.5 for commercial syntheses of vendor numbers, and 0 for vendor-affiliated or search-excerpt-only voices) and by a recency factor (1.0 for the second half of October, 0.85 for the first half, 0.7 for late September and undated current-generation configurations, 0.4 for alias-only configurations that predate the 5.5 releases, and 0 for earlier model generations). One forum thread may contribute at most 2.0 raw votes to any task-model pair, and a share qualifies only with two independent documents (or one systematic source) and a weighted score of at least 1.5. Sample sources include the Every vibe check, the Hacker News threads on GPT-6.1 Terra and GPT-6.1 Sol, the VoltAgent subagent collection, the oh-my-openagent routing guide, WidelAI, DataCamp and SlopCodeBench ([Every](https://every.to/vibe-check/vibe-check-opus-5-5-is-pulling-our-codex-converts-back-to-claude); [Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50248124); [Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49897054); [VoltAgent](https://github.com/VoltAgent/awesome-claude-code-subagents); [oh-my-openagent docs](https://ohmyopenagent.com/docs); [WidelAI](https://www.widelai.com/blog/gpt-6-1-sol-vs-claude-sonnet-5-5-vs-opus-5-5); [DataCamp](https://www.datacamp.com/blog/gpt-6-1-sol-vs-opus-5-5); [daily.dev summary of SlopCodeBench](https://daily.dev/posts/slopcodebench-10-2026-opus-5-5-astra-wins-overall-xhigh-considered-harmful-mjhhblw2w)).

Where effort is stated, configurations cluster at medium. They use low for mechanical work and reserve xhigh and max for scarce seats such as plan consultant, architect and cross-vendor reviewer.

**Two confounds.** First, harness lock-in. Claude-only subagent collections cannot name GPT models at all, and every source that puts Astra on planning works mainly in Codex, so cross-vendor shares partly reflect which tool each voice uses. Second, many stated preferences are cost-motivated rather than quality judgments: Sol, Terra and Sonnet are chosen partly for quota, and DataCamp's verdict was "if cost per task is your constraint, use GPT-6.1 Sol". The prior should therefore be small, applied only in the quality stage, halved for cross-vendor pairs, and capped where a single source swings the row.

The orchestrator treats the community share as Beta pseudo-counts on a pairwise preference. Benchmark evidence adds pseudo-counts of its own, and every measured paired run adds one more:

```text
For candidates A and B in task type t, let p = probability that A's result is preferred
in a blind paired run (or that A is accepted when B is not).

  prior   p ~ Beta(1 + m_t·ŝ_A + m_b·d_A,  1 + m_t·ŝ_B + m_b·d_B)
  m_t     = 10·w_t from Table 5; capped at 3 where one source swings the row; halved for cross-vendor pairs
  ŝ       = the two community shares renormalised to sum to 1 (5% floor for an unnamed model)
  m_b     = 15 for agreeing independent evaluators, 5 for a single independent evaluator, 0 for vendor charts
  d       = split by the discordant-pair odds of the two pass rates: pA(1−pB) : pB(1−pA)
  update  add 1 to the winner of each discordant paired run; concordant runs update usage only
  decide  quality decides if P(p > 0.5) ≥ 0.8 (or ≤ 0.2); otherwise tie → usage → headroom
  usage   û = (5·u_formula + Σ Δ%) / (5 + n); switch fully to measurement after 20 runs per
          model, effort and task type
```

**Worked examples.** The scheme behaves as intended on three rows. For scoped coding, Sonnet against Sol is a cross-vendor pair, so m is halved to 3, and their 40:32 split yields Beta(2.67, 2.33); P(p > 0.5) is about 0.57, so the pair is tied and usage decides. For planning, Opus against Astra at m = 1.5 is also tied, and the usage stage keeps Opus first, because on a Plus plan an Astra plan takes roughly an order of magnitude more of its window than an Opus plan takes of a Max 5x session. For exploration, Haiku's lopsided 84:0 share against Luna (with a 5% floor) yields Beta(2.84, 1.11), with P(p > 0.5) about 0.84, so Haiku wins on the prior alone, which agrees with Luna's independent Terminal-Bench weakness. So community evidence settles lopsided rows and orders the first experiments, but it cannot settle contested ones: Sonnet against Sol on scoped work, Opus against Astra on planning and hard tasks, and Opus against Sol on substantial work.

**How measurement overrides the prior.** After n discordant pairs, the combined prior carries (2 + m_t + m_b)/(2 + m_t + m_b + n) of the evidence. For a community-only row with m = 6, that is about a fifth after 30 discordant pairs and a tenth after 70. Usage priors fade faster: five pseudo-runs against measured window changes.

**Refreshing the prior.** Rerun the tally monthly, and replace the prior rather than accumulating it. Decay measured data with a 30-day half-life, and reset it when a model version changes. Six of the eight models in scope shipped in the last six weeks. The multipliers and n0 = 10 are judgment calls with no empirical calibration; with only about 30 voices, a single new source can move a row by 10–20 points.

### Counter-evidence

The strongest counter-evidence cuts three ways.

**Opus may deserve the everyday default more broadly than this table allows.** Anthropic ships Opus 5.5 at medium as Claude Code's default on every plan ([Claude Code model config](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/model-config)). Hacker News regulars ask what Sonnet is for, one arguing that "Opus 5.5 on Low seems smarter, cheaper, and faster than sonnet on medium" ([Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49881850)). Measurement contradicts the cost half of that claim. On CursorBench, Opus low beats Sonnet medium by 4.5 points but costs more than twice as much, and on the merged changes the two tie at 11 clean runs each, with Opus low costing 44% more.

**Sonnet may be under-used rather than over-used.** On composite indices it ties or leads Opus: 70.85% against 70.18% on the Vals Index, and first place at 92.39% on Vibe Code Bench against Opus's 90.29% in fifth ([Vals Index](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/vals_index); [Vals Vibe Code](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/vibe-code)). Anthropic's own chart has Sonnet at max scoring 70.6% on Terminal-Bench against 66.4% for Opus at xhigh ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-sonnet-5-5)), yet practitioners barely discuss it. Independent Terminal-Bench boards contradict the vendor chart, and Sonnet reaches parity only at efforts where it costs more than Opus.

**The Codex side may be better than the table credits.** Mercor's DeepSWE run reportedly ties GPT-6.1 Sol with Opus 5.5 at 72.3%, a second-hand report. Astra topped SlopCodeBench. Sol solves Terminal-Bench tasks at about one-eighth of Opus's API cost. And under a neutral harness, the Opus lead over Astra shrinks to under a point ([CodingFleet](https://codingfleet.com/blog/terminal-bench-4-leaderboard-2026/)).

**Effort and tiering have dissent too.** Anthropic's chart shows Opus rising from 57.6% at medium to 66.4% at xhigh on Terminal-Bench ([Tess](https://tess.im/blog/claude-sonnet-5-5-cheap-below-max)), which supports xhigh for hard retries. Every found that xhigh adds detail to Opus coding without excess. Against tiering itself, one commenter calls model and effort tiering "a fake lever to give to finops", and another found it "cheaper in practice to just stick with Sol" because switching models loses the cache ([Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50248124)).

**The Claude calibration may be too generous.** The provisional U5 conflicts with complaints that Opus 5.5 limits were "exhausted within 1–2 days" after mid-October ([Claude Code issue mirror](https://claudeissues.com/issue/101937-bug-opus-5-5-usage-limits-exhausted-within-1-2-days)). If sessions tightened, every Claude percentage in Table 3 is an underestimate.

### Paired runs to replace the estimates

Each comparison below should run the same task from the same base commit, in randomized order, with blind review, and record acceptance, rework, regressions under the full test suite, the served model, wall-clock time and window change. Resolving a 20-point difference in acceptance takes about 30 paired tasks; a 10-point difference takes on the order of 100. The Beta rule above allows sequential stopping.

## Conclusion

The routing question has shifted from "which vendor codes better" to "which seat, at which effort". On the strongest current evidence, effort above medium is mainly a spend dial for Opus and a liability for Sonnet on coding. The capability that justifies Opus is judgment on ambiguous work, and that shows up at medium. The practical consequence is that the orchestrator's classifier matters more than its model list. It must tell an unambiguous, testable task from one that needs intent inference. Getting that wrong in one direction wastes three to eight times the usage; in the other, it forfeits a measured 13-point quality gap.

The second shift concerns currency. If the provisional calibration holds, a $20 Claude session and a $20 Codex window hold similar numbers of tasks, even though their API-dollar contents differ about ninefold. API-price reasoning, which drove most earlier routing advice, would then systematically mislead a subscription router. The single most valuable measurement this orchestrator can make is therefore not another benchmark. It is a local calibration of each window in price-weighted units, repeated whenever the vendors move their limits. Community preference is best used to choose which experiments to run first, not to decide the routes.
